The lighthouse that outlived its empire

For sixteen centuries the Pharos of Alexandria guided ships into one of the busiest harbours in the world. It took three earthquakes to bring it down, and a fourth to make anyone go looking.

By Mira Halloran · 12 min read

The Pharos of Alexandria was commissioned by Ptolemy I Soter shortly after he took Egypt, and finished under his son around 280 BC. It stood on a small island at the mouth of the harbour, joined to the mainland by a causeway that silted up over the centuries until the island stopped being one. Estimates of its height vary between one hundred and one hundred and forty metres, which would have made it among the tallest structures built by anyone, anywhere, for the next fifteen hundred years.

What made it remarkable was not the height. It was that the thing worked, continuously, for longer than most civilisations last. A fire burned at the top through the night, and a polished bronze mirror threw the light out over the water. Ancient accounts claim the beam was visible fifty kilometres out, which is almost certainly an exaggeration, though not an absurd one.

Three storeys, three geometries

The tower was built in three stages, each a different shape: a square base, an octagonal middle, and a cylindrical top. This was not decoration. The square section housed the ramp that hauled fuel to the summit, the octagon reduced wind loading at the height where it mattered most, and the cylinder gave the lantern a uniform profile regardless of which way a ship approached from.

It was a machine for being seen, and every part of its shape was an argument about wind, fuel, and sightlines.

Fuel is the part most accounts skip. A fire bright enough to be seen from the open sea, burning every night for centuries, in a country with almost no timber. The wood came in by ship, which means the lighthouse that made the harbour usable was itself dependent on the harbour it lit.

The long collapse

Earthquakes in AD 956, 1303 and 1323 took the Pharos apart in stages. The lantern went first, then the octagonal storey, and the stump of the base survived long enough to be quarried in 1480 for the fort that still stands on the site. Nothing above ground remains.

In 1994 a French archaeological team surveying the harbour floor found several hundred tonnes of masonry, including column drums and fragments of colossal statuary, scattered across the seabed east of the fort. Some blocks weighed more than seventy tonnes. They had not been moved by anyone. They had fallen, and stayed exactly where they landed for six hundred years.

The site is now a protected underwater park, and the argument over whether to raise the blocks or leave them has been running for three decades. The case for leaving them is that the seabed has preserved them better than any museum would have. The case for raising them is that almost nobody can dive.
